Meera's mother heard the news the way she heard most difficult things — standing at the stove, back turned, as though the sambar needed her more urgently than her daughter did. "From where," she asked, not turning around. Not who. Not what does he do. From where. As if a state on a map could tell her everything else she needed to know.

Meera had rehearsed this in the car. She had rehearsed the part where her mother asked about his job, his family, whether he ate meat. She had not rehearsed the silence that followed instead, the particular quality of it, the sound of a wooden spoon set down too carefully against a steel pot.

"He's from Punjab," Meera said, and watched the word land somewhere behind her mother's shoulders. It was not a large word. It had never felt large before, said in passing about a colleague, a classmate, a stranger on a train. Said about the man she wanted to marry, it filled the whole kitchen.

"You'll learn to make roti differently," she said finally, and it took Meera a moment to realize this was not a warning. It was the beginning of an acceptance, delivered the only way her mother knew how: sideways, through the kitchen, through something that could be fixed with practice.

Two hundred kilometers away, in a flat that smelled permanently of ghee and old newspapers, Arjun was having a version of the same conversation, in a different language, with a mother who also would not turn around.
